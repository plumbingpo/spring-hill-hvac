A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Spring Hill, TN, the failures cluster at predictable points: an oxide-coated flame sensor, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Our experienced technicians diagnose a furnace the way it operates, in order, with a meter at every step. That means igniter resistance, microamp flame signal, pressure switch operation under actual inducer draft, gas valve voltage, and blower and capacitor condition. The safety checks come standard on a gas appliance: heat exchanger inspection, leak testing on the gas connections, and flue draft verification, since a hazard is not visible at the thermostat. We finish by checking temperature rise against the data plate, making sure the burner and blower work together the way the manufacturer designed them to.

Conventional or condensing, gas, propane, or electric, we work on them all, across every major brand, including the traps, drains, and dual pressure switches that come with high-efficiency models. The parts most likely to fail are stocked on every truck: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors.   • Burners light, run a few seconds, then shut down repeatedly

  • A furnace that shuts off every few minutes before the house warms up
  • Water, rust stains, or black soot around the flue or cabinet

Why Hire a Professional for Furnace Repair?

A furnace combines gas, flame, high voltage, and a flue in one cabinet, and the failure that matters most is the one you cannot see from outside: a compromised heat exchanger passing combustion gases into the air your blower pushes through the house. Delayed ignition that booms at light-off stresses that exchanger further every morning it goes unfixed. Meanwhile the diagnostic traps stack up: a furnace that trips its high limit may have a bad switch, or it may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a dying blower, and replacing the switch in the second case just removes a safety while leaving the fault.

Our experienced technicians treat every symptom as a question about the whole system. Flame signal gets measured rather than assumed, so a healthy sensor is never replaced while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve skates by. Draft is verified at the pressure switch and the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, which is how airflow problems get caught before they shorten the exchanger's life. And the gas side is leak-tested with instruments, not a sniff and a shrug. The result is a furnace that has been proven safe and correct under a full cycle, and an honest report if the unit is nearing the point where replacement beats another repair.

Maintenance Tips for Furnace Repair

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly heating and cooling problems down the road. Put these technician-approved steps to work around your property:

  • Notice how the furnace sounds at startup, because a quiet light-off is healthy and a boom is not
  • Make sure the furnace has clear combustion air; a packed utility room starves the burners
  • Replace the filter every month while the furnace is working hard, since it costs less than any other part
  • Keep storage, lint, and especially chlorine-based products away from the furnace closet
  • Test your CO detectors monthly and make sure each floor has one
  • Have the furnace checked every autumn, while a weak igniter or fouled sensor is still an easy fix
  • Keep the condensate trap and drain clear on high-efficiency units to prevent lockouts
